## The morning Alice vanished

Alice bakes the best cinnamon buns in town. Over five years she built an audience of
20,000 people on a big social app. That's where her customers find the daily menu, where
she posts the sold-out alerts, where her regulars say hi.

One Tuesday she opens the app and sees a grey box: *"Your account has been suspended."*
No human to call. No list of her followers to export. An automated filter decided
something, and five years of relationships went quiet in a single click.

Nobody had to hack Alice. Nobody had to argue with her. The company simply **owned the
room** she was talking in, and it switched off her microphone.

<Callout locale="en" tone="info" testid="ch01-callout-not-just-bans" title="It's not only about bans">
The same single point of control also decides **what you see** (the algorithm), **what it
costs** (fees, ads), and **whether the place exists at all** (shutdowns, acquisitions,
outages). A ban is just the most dramatic version of the problem.
</Callout>

## Four ways to wire up a social network

Let's take the same five friends (Alice, Bob, Carol, Dave and Erin) and connect them four
different ways. Your job is to be the chaos monkey: knock servers offline, then press
**Ban Alice** and see who can still be heard.

- **Centralized**: one company, one server, everyone lives there.
- **Federated**: many independent servers that relay posts to each other. This is the
  <Term id="federation" locale="en">federation</Term> model of Mastodon and email.
- **Nostr**: simple <Term id="relay" locale="en">relays</Term> that store and forward
  notes, and people who post to *several* relays at once.
- **Bluesky**: a newer design where your data lives on a personal data server (PDS), and big
  aggregators stitch everyone's posts into timelines.

Try this order: kill **BigCo** in Centralized, **tea.social** in Federated, **alpha** in
Nostr, and the **AppView** in Bluesky. Then flip back and press **Ban Alice** in each one.
Notice the pattern?

## What just happened?

**Centralized** is a single trunk: chop it and the whole tree falls. Ban Alice and she is
simply gone, because the company *is* the network.

**Federated** is a lot better. Losing one instance only hurts the people who live there.
But look at Alice's scoreboard row: *"Account gone with tea.social."* In a federated world
your name looks like `@alice@tea.social`, and that last half is the landlord. If the
instance shuts down, or the admin bans you, your account and your follower list go with
it. You can move house, but only while the old landlord is still answering the door.

**Nostr** shrugs. Alice's identity is not an account on anyone's server: it's a
<Term id="keypair" locale="en">keypair</Term> that lives on her own device. Every note she
writes is <Term id="signature" locale="en">signed</Term> with her secret key, so *any*
relay can store it and *any* reader can check it really came from her. She sends each note
to two relays. If one dies, or bans her, the other still has it.

**Bluesky** sits in between. Accounts can move between data servers, which is great. But
in practice most people read through one big relay and one big AppView, so knocking out
either one still darkens almost everything today.

## Why relays are dumb on purpose

Here's the trick that makes Nostr work: relays **don't talk to each other** and **don't own
anyone**. A relay is a small server that accepts signed notes (called
<Term id="event" locale="en">events</Term>) over a <Term id="websocket" locale="en">WebSocket</Term>
and hands them back to whoever asks.

Think of relays like **post office boxes** rather than a town square. Alice rents a box at
two post offices and drops a copy of every letter into both. Her friends' mailmen (their
<Term id="client" locale="en">clients</Term>) know which post offices she uses and go pick
up her letters there. If one post office burns down or refuses her business, the letters
are still waiting at the other one, and Alice can open a third box tomorrow without asking
anyone's permission.

That's the whole recipe:

1. **You are your keys.** No sign-up, no username to confiscate. Your public key
   (<Term id="npub" locale="en">npub</Term>) is your name.
2. **Every note is signed.** Relays can't forge or quietly edit what you said.
3. **Publish everywhere.** You pick several relays, and you can change them any time.
4. **Readers follow you.** Clients use your published relay list to find your notes:
   that's the <Term id="outbox-model" locale="en">outbox model</Term>.

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch01-uth-event" locale="en" title="Under the hood: what a relay actually receives">

Everything in Nostr is one data shape, defined in [NIP-01](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/01.md).
A client sends it to a relay inside an `EVENT` message over a WebSocket:

```json
["EVENT", {
  "id": "<32-byte sha256 of the serialized event, hex>",
  "pubkey": "<Alice's 32-byte public key, hex>",
  "created_at": 1735689600,
  "kind": 1,
  "tags": [],
  "content": "Cinnamon buns are back at 8am!",
  "sig": "<64-byte Schnorr signature of the id, hex>"
}]
```

The relay answers with `["OK", "<event id>", true, ""]` if it stored the note, or `false`
plus a reason (for example `"blocked: ..."` or `"rate-limited: ..."`) if it refuses. Note
what's *missing*: there's no account id, no password, no server name in Alice's identity.
The `pubkey` and `sig` are all a relay (or reader) needs to know who wrote it.

</Drawer>

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch01-uth-publish" locale="en" title="Under the hood: publishing to several relays with nostr-tools">

Redundancy is just a loop. With [nostr-tools](https://github.com/nbd-wtf/nostr-tools):

```ts
import { finalizeEvent, generateSecretKey } from "nostr-tools/pure";
import { SimplePool } from "nostr-tools/pool";

const sk = generateSecretKey(); // your identity: 32 random bytes, never leaves the device
const note = finalizeEvent(
  { kind: 1, created_at: Math.floor(Date.now() / 1000), tags: [], content: "hello, relays!" },
  sk, // computes pubkey, id and signature
);

const pool = new SimplePool();
const relays = ["wss://relay.alpha.example", "wss://relay.beta.example"];
// One promise per relay: it's enough that *some* relay accepts the note.
await Promise.any(pool.publish(relays, note));
```

A relay that rejects the note can't change it or claim it as its own; it can only decline
to store it.

</Drawer>

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch01-uth-outbox" locale="en" title="Under the hood: the outbox model (NIP-65)">

How do Bob's client and Alice's relays find each other? Alice publishes a small
replaceable event of kind `10002` listing her relays, as described in
[NIP-65](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/65.md):

```json
{
  "kind": 10002,
  "tags": [
    ["r", "wss://relay.alpha.example"],
    ["r", "wss://relay.beta.example", "write"],
    ["r", "wss://relay.gamma.example", "read"]
  ],
  "content": "",
  "...": "pubkey, created_at, id, sig as usual"
}
```

An `r` tag without a marker means "read *and* write". To show Alice's notes, Bob's client
fetches her kind `10002` and opens subscriptions on her **write** relays; to reach Alice
(replies, mentions), it publishes to her **read** relays. That's why the sandbox lets any
reader pull from any relay Alice writes to: the client goes to the author, not the other
way around.

</Drawer>

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch01-uth-compare" locale="en" title="Under the hood: Mastodon and Bluesky, a bit more precisely">

- **Mastodon / ActivityPub.** Your identity is `@name@instance`, resolved through the
  instance's WebFinger endpoint, and your posts are signed with keys **the instance holds**.
  Account migration exists (a `Move` activity redirects followers), but it needs the old
  instance to still be online and cooperative.
- **Bluesky / AT Protocol.** Your identity is a DID (usually `did:plc`) that points at your
  current PDS, and your posts live in a signed repository there. Accounts can migrate
  between PDSes, which is real portability. Timelines, though, come from relays that crawl
  every PDS and AppViews that index them; anyone may run one, but most traffic today goes
  through a few large ones.
- **Nostr.** The identity *is* the signing key and every event carries its own signature,
  so there's nothing to migrate: you just point at different relays.

The sandbox's scoring (see `sandbox.ts` in this chapter's components) treats a conversation
as alive when an author's post can travel to a reader through online, non-banning
infrastructure, and people never relay for each other.

</Drawer>

## Is this just "no moderation"?

No, and that's an important nuance. Each relay is free to set its own rules: block spam,
require payment (a <Term id="paid-relay" locale="en">paid relay</Term>), or only accept
members of a community. Each client can filter what you see, for example using your
<Term id="web-of-trust" locale="en">web of trust</Term>.

What Nostr removes is the **single** chokepoint. Moderation becomes a choice made by many
relays and many apps, instead of a verdict from one company that also happens to hold your
identity. That property has a name:
<Term id="censorship-resistance" locale="en">censorship resistance</Term>.

<Callout locale="en" tone="warning" testid="ch01-callout-tradeoff" title="Nothing is free">
Owning your identity means owning the risk: if you lose your secret key there's no
"forgot password" button (we'll cover <Term id="key-loss" locale="en">key loss</Term> and
how to protect yourself later). And with many independent relays, there's no global
search or single "view count" to check. Chapter 12 is all about these trade-offs.
</Callout>

## Where did Nostr come from?

Nostr stands for **"Notes and Other Stuff Transmitted by Relays."** It was proposed in 2020
by a developer known as fiatjaf, as a deliberately tiny protocol: a JSON event format, a
handful of WebSocket messages, and public-key signatures. Everything else is added
through optional, numbered proposals called <Term id="nip" locale="en">NIPs</Term>
(Nostr Implementation Possibilities), maintained openly at
[github.com/nostr-protocol/nips](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips).

The rest of this course is a tour of that recipe. Next up: the keypair that *is* you.
