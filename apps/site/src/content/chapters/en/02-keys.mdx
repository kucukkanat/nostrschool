## No sign-up form. Ever.

Every app you've used starts the same way: pick a username, choose a password, confirm your email.
Behind the scenes, a company writes you into **its** database. Your account lives there, and it
exists only as long as they say so.

Nostr skips all of that. Your identity is not a row in someone's database: it's a
<Term id="keypair" locale="en">keypair</Term>, two big numbers that belong together:

- a **secret key** (<Term id="privkey" locale="en">private key</Term>): 32 random bytes that only you know, and
- a **public key** (<Term id="pubkey" locale="en">pubkey</Term>): computed from the secret key, and safe to show the world.

Think of a mailbox with a slot. The **public key** is the address painted on the front: you put it
on business cards so people can find you. The **secret key** is the only key that opens the box,
and here it does something even better: it lets you stamp letters with a seal nobody else can
forge.

No server hands these out. You make them yourself, on your own device, in a fraction of a second.
Let's do that right now.

<KeyForge client:visible locale="en" />

Press the button a few times. Every keypair is brand new: there are about 2<sup>256</sup> possible
secret keys, so the chance of two people ever rolling the same one is effectively zero. No
registry is needed to keep keys unique: math does it for free.

  or sent anywhere, but treat them as throwaway: don't use them for a real identity.

## Why can't anyone work out my secret?

The public key is calculated *from* the secret key using an elliptic curve called
<Term id="secp256k1" locale="en">secp256k1</Term> (the same one Bitcoin uses). The useful property is
that this calculation is a **one-way street**: going forward takes microseconds, going backward is
hopeless.

Here's a toy version you can actually see. Your secret is a number of hops; the public key is where
you land on the clock. Try it, then try to work backwards.

<OneWayClock client:visible locale="en" />

On a 61-spot clock, guessing backwards takes a blink. On the real curve there are about
10<sup>77</sup> spots. Every computer on Earth guessing for the lifetime of the universe would
not make a dent. That's what lets you publish your public key everywhere and sleep soundly.

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch02-uth-curve" locale="en" title="Under the hood: secp256k1 & x-only keys">

The real operation is **scalar multiplication on an elliptic curve**: `P = k · G`, where `k` is your
secret key (a 256-bit integer between 1 and the curve order *n* − 1), `G` is the curve's fixed
generator point, and `P` is the public point. Recovering `k` from `P` is the *elliptic-curve
discrete logarithm problem*, which has no known efficient solution.

[NIP-01](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/01.md) says keys and signatures follow
[BIP-340](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0340.mediawiki) (Schnorr signatures on
secp256k1). BIP-340 uses **x-only** public keys: only the 32-byte x coordinate is kept, and the y
coordinate is implied to be even. That's why a Nostr `pubkey` is exactly 64 lowercase hex
characters, not the 66-character "compressed" keys you may know from elsewhere.

```ts
import { generateSecretKey, getPublicKey } from "nostr-tools/pure";

const sk = generateSecretKey(); // Uint8Array(32), from a CSPRNG
const pk = getPublicKey(sk); // 64 lowercase hex chars (x-only)
console.log(pk);
```

Not every 32-byte value is a valid key: the secret must be below *n*
(≈ 1.158 × 10<sup>77</sup>), and a public key must be an x coordinate that is actually on the curve.

</Drawer>

## npub and nsec: same keys, friendlier spelling

Raw keys are written in **hex**: 64 characters of `0–9` and `a–f`. Computers love hex. Humans
copy-pasting it into a chat… less so. Mix up one character and you've silently pointed at someone
else (or no one).

So <Term id="nip19" locale="en">NIP-19</Term> re-spells keys using <Term id="bech32" locale="en">bech32</Term>:

- your public key becomes an <Term id="npub" locale="en">npub</Term>: `npub1…`
- your secret key becomes an <Term id="nsec" locale="en">nsec</Term>: `nsec1…`

The prefix tells you **what** you're holding (so you notice when you're about to paste a secret),
the alphabet skips look-alike characters like `1`, `b`, `i` and `o`, and the last six characters are
a **checksum**: change any character and software will refuse it instead of quietly using the wrong
key. Scroll back up to the forge and step through the encoding: the bytes are regrouped 5 bits at a
time, and each 5-bit number picks one letter from a 32-letter alphabet.

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch02-uth-bech32" locale="en" title="Under the hood: NIP-19 encoding">

1. Take the 32 payload bytes (256 bits).
2. Regroup them into **5-bit words**: 256 / 5 = 51.2, so 52 words, the last one padded with zero bits.
3. Compute a 6-word **BCH checksum** over the prefix (the *human-readable part*, `npub`) and the words.
4. Map all 58 words through the alphabet `qpzry9x8gf2tvdw0s3jn54khce6mua7l` and glue it together:
   `hrp + "1" + chars`. Result: 63 characters for every npub and nsec.

Bech32 strings are **for humans only**. [NIP-19](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/19.md)
is explicit: they must not be used inside events or relay messages. On the wire, it's always hex
(event trimmed to the interesting field):

```json
{
  "kind": 1,
  "pubkey": "7e7e9c42a91bfef19fa929e5fda1b72e0ebc1a4c1141673e2794234d86addf4e",
  "content": "gm"
}
```

```ts
import * as nip19 from "nostr-tools/nip19";

const npub = nip19.npubEncode(pk); // "npub1…"
const nsec = nip19.nsecEncode(sk); // "nsec1…" (sk is a Uint8Array)
const { type, data } = nip19.decode(npub); // type === "npub", data === pk (hex)
```

NIP-19 also defines `note` (an event id), plus "shareable identifiers" that bundle extra hints as
**TLV** (type-length-value) records: <Term id="nprofile" locale="en">nprofile</Term> (pubkey + relays),
<Term id="nevent" locale="en">nevent</Term> (event id + relays, optional author and kind) and
<Term id="naddr" locale="en">naddr</Term> (kind + author + `d` tag of an addressable event). Try them
all in the standalone <a href={href("en", "tools/keys")} data-testid="ch02-tool-link">key tool</a>.

Two related NIPs: [NIP-49](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/49.md) defines
<Term id="ncryptsec" locale="en">`ncryptsec`</Term>, a password-encrypted secret key and the recommended backup format, and
[NIP-06](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/06.md) derives keys from a BIP-39 seed
phrase (path `m/44'/1237'/<account>'/0/0`). NIP-06 is now marked *unrecommended* in the NIPs index
in favor of a single nsec, so treat it as legacy you may meet in older wallets.

</Drawer>

## Your key is your pen

Holding a secret key isn't just about *being* someone: it's about *proving* it. Every post you
publish is a little JSON <Term id="event" locale="en">event</Term> carrying your public key and a
<Term id="signature" locale="en">signature</Term>, made with your secret key using the
<Term id="schnorr" locale="en">Schnorr</Term> scheme. Anyone (any <Term id="relay" locale="en">relay</Term>,
any app, any stranger) can check that signature with nothing but your public key. Nobody can fake
it without your secret.

Try it with the key you forged above. Then change a single letter.

<SignatureStamp client:visible locale="en" />

That's why Nostr doesn't need a trusted server: the proof travels *with* the message. We'll take a
signed event apart byte by byte in the next chapter.

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch02-uth-schnorr" locale="en" title="Under the hood: what actually gets signed">

Nostr never signs your raw text. The event is serialized as
`[0, pubkey, created_at, kind, tags, content]`, hashed with <Term id="sha256" locale="en">SHA-256</Term>
to get the 32-byte `id`, and **the id** is signed with BIP-340 Schnorr, producing a 64-byte `sig`
(128 hex characters). Change the content and the id changes, so the old signature no longer matches.

```ts
import { finalizeEvent, verifyEvent } from "nostr-tools/pure";

const event = finalizeEvent(
  { kind: 1, created_at: Math.floor(Date.now() / 1000), tags: [], content: "gm" },
  sk,
); // adds pubkey, id and sig
verifyEvent(event); // true
```

</Drawer>

## Guard the nsec with your life (well, almost)

Here's the flip side of "no company owns your account": **no company can rescue it either**.

- Anyone who gets your nsec **is** you. They can post, follow and DM as you, forever, and you can't
  change "your password", because there isn't one.
- If you lose your nsec with no backup, the identity is gone. That's <Term id="key-loss" locale="en">key loss</Term>,
  and it's one of Nostr's honest trade-offs (more in the last chapter).

Your npub, on the other hand, is meant to be shared: on your website, in your bio, on a T-shirt.
Let's practice telling the two apart.

<Callout locale="en" tone="danger" testid="ch02-never-share">
  Real apps never need you to paste your nsec. Use a <Term id="signer" locale="en">signer</Term>
  instead: a browser extension (<Term id="nip07" locale="en">NIP-07</Term>) or a remote
  <Term id="bunker" locale="en">bunker</Term> (<Term id="nip46" locale="en">NIP-46</Term>) keeps the
  secret key in one place and only hands out signatures. Chapter 10 shows how.
</Callout>

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch02-uth-hygiene" locale="en" title="Under the hood: key hygiene for developers">

- Generate keys with a CSPRNG (`generateSecretKey()` uses `crypto.getRandomValues`), never with
  `Math.random()` or a hash of something guessable.
- Keep secret keys as `Uint8Array` in memory, and don't log them. Prefer delegating to a signer:
  `window.nostr.signEvent()` ([NIP-07](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/07.md)) or a
  `bunker://` connection ([NIP-46](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/46.md)).
- For backups, offer `ncryptsec` ([NIP-49](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/49.md))
  instead of plain nsec text. Skip NIP-06 seed phrases for new code: the NIPs index marks them
  unrecommended in favor of a single nsec.
- Accept both hex and NIP-19 input, but store and transmit **hex**; decode `nostr:` URIs
  ([NIP-21](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/21.md)) with the same NIP-19 decoder.

</Drawer>
