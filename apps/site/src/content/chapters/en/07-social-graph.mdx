## Your friends list is a note you sign

On a big platform, "who follows whom" is a giant table inside the company's database. You
can look at your row, but you don't own it, and you can't take it with you.

Nostr has no such table. Instead, each person publishes their own
<Term id="follow-list" locale="en">follow list</Term>: a small, signed
<Term id="event" locale="en">event</Term> of <Term id="kind" locale="en">kind</Term> `3` that
says "these are the people I follow." Each follow is one line, a `p`
<Term id="tag" locale="en">tag</Term> holding the other person's
<Term id="pubkey" locale="en">public key</Term>.

Think of it like the **contacts page in your own notebook**. Nobody else writes in it. You
can photocopy it and leave copies at several <Term id="relay" locale="en">relays</Term>, and
any app you open later can read it and rebuild your timeline. New phone? New app? Your
friends come with you, because the list was never the app's in the first place.

Put everyone's notebook pages side by side and you get a **social graph**. Here are our
seven fixture friends, drawn from their real (signed!) kind 3 events:

Try this: hover **Grace** with the "Who follows them" lens on. Only Carol and Erin follow

<Callout locale="en" tone="tip" testid="ch07-callout-followers" title="Wait, where are the follower lists?">
There aren't any! Your kind 3 lists who **you** follow. "Who follows me" is a question an
app answers by *searching* relays for kind 3 events that contain your pubkey. Different
apps, asking different relays, can come up with different follower counts. That's normal on
Nostr, and why a follower count is an estimate, not a fact.
</Callout>

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch07-uth-kind3" locale="en" title="Under the hood: a kind 3 event (NIP-02)">

[NIP-02](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/02.md) defines the follow list.
Every follow is a `p` tag: `["p", <pubkey hex>, <relay hint>, <petname>]`. The relay hint and
the petname (a local nickname) are optional and can be empty strings. `content` is not used.

```json
{
  "kind": 3,
  "pubkey": "<Grace's pubkey>",
  "created_at": 1735682400,
  "tags": [
    ["p", "e550c6e9…44cc", "wss://relay.alpha.example", "alice"],
    ["p", "a7388391…e183", "wss://relay.beta.example", "bob"],
    ["p", "c7175000…5deb", "wss://relay.alpha.example", "erin"]
  ],
  "content": "",
  "id": "…",
  "sig": "…"
}
```

Building the graph is just reading tags. With [nostr-tools](https://github.com/nbd-wtf/nostr-tools):

```ts
import { SimplePool } from "nostr-tools/pool";

const pool = new SimplePool();
const relays = ["wss://relay.alpha.example"];

// Who does Grace follow? Her newest kind 3.
const list = await pool.get(relays, { kinds: [3], authors: [gracePubkey] });
const follows = (list?.tags ?? []).filter((t) => t[0] === "p").map((t) => t[1]);

// Who follows Grace? Search for kind 3 events that tag her (an estimate!).
const followers = await pool.querySync(relays, { kinds: [3], "#p": [gracePubkey] });
```

Clients append new follows **to the end** of the list, so the tag order roughly records
when you followed each person.

</Drawer>

## Replaceable means "the newest copy wins"

Kind 3 is a <Term id="replaceable-event" locale="en">replaceable event</Term>. A relay keeps
only the **newest** follow list per person and throws older ones away. That keeps things
tidy, but it has a sharp edge: a new list doesn't add to the old one, it **replaces it
whole**.

So what happens if an app that hasn't synced in a month publishes your list? Let's find out.
You're Grace. Follow a few people, publish from your phone, then let that dusty old tablet
publish its own (outdated) idea of your list:

<ReplaceableTrap client:visible locale="en" />

Ouch. This isn't a theoretical bug: people have lost their whole follow list this way. Good
apps always **fetch your newest kind 3 right before editing it**, which is exactly what the
"Sync phone from relay" button does.

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch07-uth-replaceable" locale="en" title="Under the hood: how relays pick the winner (NIP-01)">

[NIP-01](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/01.md) defines kinds `0`, `3` and
`10000`–`19999` as replaceable: for each `pubkey` + `kind`, a relay is only expected to keep
the latest event. "Latest" means the highest `created_at`; if two events share the same
timestamp, the one with the **lowest `id`** (first in lexical order) is kept. The demo uses
exactly that rule:

```ts
const newest = (events: NostrEvent[]) =>
  events.reduce((best, e) =>
    e.created_at > best.created_at ||
    (e.created_at === best.created_at && e.id < best.id) ? e : best);
```

The events in the demo are really signed with Grace's fixture key (it's public on purpose),
so you could paste one into the Event inspector and it would verify.

</Drawer>

## But where do I find my friends' notes?

Here's a puzzle. Erin follows Alice, Carol, Frank and Grace. Her app wants to show their
latest notes. But there are thousands of relays, and nobody is on all of them. Which ones
should the app ask?

The naive answer is "everyone's on the big relay, just ask that one." That works... until
someone isn't. The better answer is the <Term id="outbox-model" locale="en">outbox model</Term>,
described in <Term id="nip65" locale="en">NIP-65</Term>.

Every person publishes one more small event, kind `10002`, listing their relays, each one
marked as:

- **write**: "I publish my notes here." This is their **outbox**. Go here to *read* them.
- **read**: "I check here for replies and mentions." This is their **inbox**. Go here to
  *reach* them.
- no marker: both.

It's like a friend telling you "I post my letters from the Alpha and Beta post offices, and
I collect my mail at Gamma." To read their letters you visit Alpha or Beta. To write to them,
you drop your letter at Gamma.

Press play and watch Erin's <Term id="client" locale="en">client</Term> work it out. Then
switch to **Everyone on one relay** and compare. And try **Reply to a friend** to see the
inbox side.

<OutboxRouter client:visible locale="en" />

Notice two things:

1. In the outbox model the app opens a <Term id="subscription" locale="en">subscription</Term>
   on each relay asking **only** for the authors who write there. Nobody goes missing, even
   Grace, who only uses tiny Gamma.
2. With one relay, Frank and Grace silently vanish. Nobody gets an error; Erin just never
   sees their notes. That's the real danger: the network *looks* fine.

<Callout locale="en" tone="info" testid="ch07-callout-why" title="Why this matters for freedom">
If every app assumed one big relay, that relay would quietly become the new central
platform: whoever it bans disappears for everyone. The outbox model is what lets you pick
small, paid, or self-hosted relays without becoming invisible. It's a big part of Nostr's
<Term id="censorship-resistance" locale="en">censorship resistance</Term>.
</Callout>

<Drawer client:visible testid="ch07-uth-nip65" locale="en" title="Under the hood: kind 10002 and the NIP-65 rules">

[NIP-65](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/65.md) defines kind `10002`, a
replaceable event whose `r` tags list relays. A marker of `read` or `write` narrows a relay to
one direction; no marker means both. Carol's list from our fixtures:

```json
{
  "kind": 10002,
  "tags": [
    ["r", "wss://relay.gamma.example"],
    ["r", "wss://relay.alpha.example", "write"]
  ],
  "content": ""
}
```

The rules, in short:

- To download events **from** a user, use that user's **write** relays.
- To download events **about** a user (where they're tagged), use that user's **read** relays.
- When publishing, send the event to the **author's write relays** and to the **read relays of
  every tagged user**.
- Spread your own kind `10002` widely, including well-known "indexer" relays that apps ask for
  relay lists, and keep it small: 2–4 relays per direction.

The demo builds one `REQ` per relay with only the authors who write there:

```ts
import { SimplePool } from "nostr-tools/pool";

const pool = new SimplePool();
const INDEXERS = ["wss://relay.alpha.example"];

// 1. Where does each followed person write?
const lists = await pool.querySync(INDEXERS, { kinds: [10002], authors: follows });
const writeRelaysOf = new Map(
  lists.map((e) => [
    e.pubkey,
    e.tags.filter((t) => t[0] === "r" && t[2] !== "read").map((t) => t[1]),
  ]),
);

// 2. Group authors by relay, then subscribe per relay with just those authors.
const byRelay = new Map<string, string[]>();
for (const [author, relays] of writeRelaysOf)
  for (const url of relays) byRelay.set(url, [...(byRelay.get(url) ?? []), author]);

for (const [url, authors] of byRelay)
  pool.subscribeMany([url], { kinds: [1], authors, limit: 20 }, { onevent: show });
```

Real clients add guardrails: cap connections per author (two or three relays is plenty),
cache relay lists, and pick a small set that still covers everyone. The demo's "fewest relays"
counter solves that covering problem exactly; with hundreds of relays, apps approximate it.

For replies, a `p` tag (and the `e` tag of the note being replied to, per
[NIP-10](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/10.md)) tells everyone who should
be notified, and the outbox rules tell the app *where* to deliver it.

</Drawer>

## Beyond follows

The follow graph is useful for more than your timeline. Apps use it as a
<Term id="web-of-trust" locale="en">web of trust</Term>: "show replies from people my
friends follow, hide strangers" is a surprisingly strong spam filter, and nobody had to
build a central reputation database to get it. Your kind 3 also doubles as a portable
backup and a list of relay hints for the people you follow.

That's the trick of this chapter: a social network built entirely from small notes that each
person signs, stores where they like, and can take anywhere.
